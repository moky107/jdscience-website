The common idea across A12.1

 • Reaction rate depends on how frequently successful collisions occur.
 • Surface area changes how many solid particles are exposed for collisions.
 • Concentration changes how many reacting particles occupy a given volume of
 solution.
 • Pressure changes how closely gaseous particles are packed in a given volume.
 • Each factor can therefore change collision frequency.

A12.1.1 Surface area: what changes?

 • For the same mass of a solid, smaller pieces expose a greater total surface
 area.
 • Only particles at the exposed surface are directly available for collisions with
 another reactant.
 • Powder or small chips therefore expose more reacting particles than one large
 piece of the same mass.
 • The amount of solid can remain the same while its exposed surface area
 changes.





A12.1 Factors affecting rates of chemical reactions

## Page 7

Surface area and collision theory

 • Greater surface area → more solid particles exposed.
 • More exposed particles → more opportunities for reactant particles to collide
 with the solid each second.
 • Collision frequency increases.
 • Successful collisions occur more frequently, so reaction rate increases.

## Page 8

Model example: large chips versus powder

 • Imagine equal masses of the same solid reacting under identical conditions.
 • Large chips: lower total exposed surface area → fewer collisions at the solid
 surface per unit time.
 • Powder: greater total exposed surface area → more collisions at the surface
 per unit time.
 • Prediction: the powder reacts faster.

Surface area investigation: design

 • Purpose: compare reaction rate for the same mass of solid at different particle sizes.
 • Independent variable: particle size/surface area.
 • Dependent variable: a measure of reaction rate, for example gas volume produced
 over time or time to a defined endpoint.
 • Controls may include mass of solid, reactant concentration/volume, temperature and
 apparatus.
 • Use centre-approved laboratory procedures and risk assessment for the selected
 reaction.

## Page 10

Surface area investigation: interpreting results

 • A faster reaction may show a steeper initial gas-volume versus time curve.
 • If the same limiting amount reacts, the final amount of product may be the
 same even though it is reached sooner.
 • Repeat measurements improve confidence in the pattern.
 • An anomalous result should be investigated rather than automatically
 discarded.

A12.1.2 Concentration: particle meaning

 • Concentration describes how much dissolved reactant is present in a given
 volume of solution.
 • At higher concentration, there are more reactant particles in the same volume.
 • The particles are therefore more likely to encounter one another.
 • This changes collision frequency.





A12.1 Factors affecting rates of chemical reactions

## Page 14

Concentration and collision theory

 • Higher concentration → more reactant particles per unit volume.
 • Particles are closer together on average / there are more possible encounters
 in the same space.
 • Collision frequency increases.
 • More successful collisions occur per unit time, so reaction rate increases.

## Page 15

Model example: dilute versus concentrated

 • Compare equal volumes at the same temperature.
 • Dilute solution: fewer reactant particles in the volume → lower collision
 frequency.
 • More concentrated solution: more reactant particles in the same volume →
 higher collision frequency.
 • Provided other variables are controlled, the more concentrated reactant gives
 a faster reaction.

Concentration investigation: design

 • Purpose: compare reaction rate at different reactant concentrations.
 • Independent variable: concentration of the selected reactant.
 • Dependent variable: a suitable rate measurement.
 • Control variables may include temperature, volumes of other reactants, surface
 area/mass of any solid and apparatus.
 • Only one intended factor should be changed for a valid comparison.

A12.1.3 Pressure: when it matters

 • Pressure is relevant here to gaseous reactants.
 • Increasing the pressure of a gas at constant temperature can place the same
 number of gas particles into a smaller volume.
 • The gas particles are closer together.
 • They collide more frequently.





A12.1 Factors affecting rates of chemical reactions

## Page 20

Pressure and collision theory

 • Higher pressure of gaseous reactants → more particles per unit volume /
 particles closer together.
 • Collision frequency increases.
 • More collisions means more opportunities for successful collisions each
 second.
 • Reaction rate therefore increases.





A12.1 Factors affecting rates of chemical reactions

## Page 21

Model: compressing gaseous reactants

 • Before compression: gas particles occupy a larger volume and collide less
 frequently.
 • After compression: the same particles occupy a smaller volume.
 • Particle spacing decreases and collision frequency increases.
 • At unchanged temperature, the explanation is primarily increased collision
 frequency rather than increased average kinetic energy.

## Page 28

Common misconceptions

 • ‘Smaller particles contain more substance.’ Incorrect: the same mass can be divided
 into smaller pieces; total amount need not change.
 • ‘Higher concentration makes particles move faster.’ Incorrect if temperature is
 unchanged; there are more particles per unit volume.
 • ‘Higher gas pressure always means higher temperature.’ Incorrect: pressure can be
 increased by reducing volume at constant temperature.
 • ‘More collisions automatically means every collision is successful.’ Incorrect: successful
 collisions still require sufficient energy and correct orientation.

## Page 29

Practical quality: making comparisons valid

 • Change only the intended independent variable.
 • Keep relevant control variables constant.
 • Use the same method and endpoint for compared trials.
 • Repeat measurements where appropriate to assess repeatability.
 • Consider measurement uncertainty and anomalous results when interpreting
 small differences.
