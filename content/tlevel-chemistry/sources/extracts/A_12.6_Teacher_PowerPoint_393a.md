The Haber process: the industrial reaction


    N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)  ΔH < 0 (forward reaction is exothermic)





    Industrial aim: make ammonia at a useful rate and yield, at an economically
   acceptable cost.

## Page 6

The industrial conditions

 • Temperature: about 450 °C.
 • Pressure: about 200 atm.
 • Catalyst: iron.
 • Unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen can be separated from ammonia and
 recycled.
 • These conditions are not chosen to maximise one factor alone: they represent
 an industrial compromise.

## Page 7

Temperature and equilibrium yield

 • The forward reaction is exothermic.
 • Lower temperature favours the exothermic forward reaction, so the equilibrium yield
 of ammonia increases.
 • Higher temperature favours the endothermic reverse reaction, so the equilibrium yield
 of ammonia decreases.
 • From equilibrium alone, a low temperature would appear desirable.





A12.6 Haber process

## Page 8

Temperature and reaction rate

 • At lower temperature, particles have lower average kinetic energy.
 • Collisions occur less energetically and a smaller proportion can overcome the
 activation energy.
 • The reaction is therefore slower.
 • At higher temperature, the reaction is faster, but the equilibrium yield of ammonia is
 lower.

## Page 10

Why about 450 °C?

 • A very low temperature would give a higher equilibrium yield but an
 impractically slow reaction rate.
 • A very high temperature would give a fast rate but a lower equilibrium yield of
 ammonia.
 • About 450 °C provides a sufficiently fast reaction with a useful equilibrium
 yield.
 • This is a compromise between rate and equilibrium yield.

## Page 11

Pressure and equilibrium

 • N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g).
 • There are 4 moles of gas on the reactant side and 2 on the product side.
 • Increasing pressure shifts equilibrium toward the side with fewer gas moles.
 • Therefore, increasing pressure favours ammonia formation and increases
 equilibrium yield.





A12.6 Haber process

## Page 12

Why not use an extremely high pressure?

 • Higher pressure can increase the equilibrium yield of ammonia.
 • But generating and containing very high pressure requires more energy and stronger,
 more expensive equipment.
 • Higher pressure can therefore increase capital and operating costs and introduces
 greater engineering demands.
 • Industry uses a high but economically practical pressure rather than the maximum
 possible pressure.





A12.6 Haber process

## Page 13

Why about 200 atm?

 • Pressure must be high enough to give a useful increase in ammonia equilibrium yield.
 • Beyond a point, the extra yield benefit must be weighed against increased
 compression, equipment and operating costs.
 • About 200 atm is commonly used as an industrial compromise.
 • The exact operating conditions can vary between plants; the specification focus is the
 compromise.

## Page 14

The iron catalyst

 • Iron provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy.
 • This increases the rate at which equilibrium is reached.
 • The catalyst does not increase the equilibrium yield of ammonia.
 • It allows a useful reaction rate without relying on an even higher temperature,
 which would reduce the equilibrium yield.





A12.6 Haber process

## Page 15

Catalyst: rate versus equilibrium

 • With a catalyst, both forward and reverse reactions are accelerated.
 • Equilibrium is reached faster.
 • The equilibrium position and equilibrium composition at a fixed temperature
 and pressure are unchanged.
 • This distinction is essential when explaining the Haber process.

## Page 17

Industrial comparison table

  • Low temperature: higher NH₃ equilibrium yield, but slow rate.
  • High temperature: faster rate, but lower NH₃ equilibrium yield.
  • Low pressure: lower equipment/compression cost, but lower NH₃ equilibrium yield.
  • Very high pressure: higher yield, but greater equipment/compression cost.
  • Catalyst: faster approach to equilibrium without changing equilibrium position.

## Page 18

Worked decision example

  • Option A: 300 °C, 200 atm — high equilibrium yield, but reaction is too slow for efficient
 production.
  • Option B: 650 °C, 200 atm — fast reaction, but equilibrium yield of NH₃ is reduced.
  • Option C: 450 °C, 200 atm with iron catalyst — useful yield and useful rate.
  • Industrial decisions balance production rate, yield and economic constraints rather than
 maximising one variable.

## Page 21

Decision challenge: indicative answer


    • A defensible industrial choice is around 450 °C and 200 atm with an iron catalyst.
    • 350 °C would favour a higher equilibrium yield but the reaction rate would be
   slower.
    • 600 °C would increase rate but reduce the equilibrium yield of ammonia.
    • 400 atm could increase yield further but requires more compression and stronger,
  more costly equipment.
    • 100 atm reduces pressure-related cost but gives a lower equilibrium yield than
   200 atm.

## Page 23

Error detective: answers


    • Lowest temperature: ignores the very slow reaction rate.
    • Highest pressure: ignores energy, equipment and operating costs and
   engineering demands.
    • Iron catalyst: increases rate and speeds attainment of equilibrium; it does not
  change equilibrium position/yield at fixed conditions.
    • Higher temperature: increases rate but, for the exothermic Haber reaction,
   reduces the equilibrium yield of ammonia.
