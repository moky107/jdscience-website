A12.3.1 Enthalpy, H

 • Enthalpy, H, is the energy content of a system at constant pressure.
 • In reaction chemistry, we usually compare the enthalpy of reactants and
 products rather than measuring an absolute H value directly.
 • H is a state quantity: reactants and products can be represented at different
 enthalpy levels on a diagram.





A12.3 Enthalpy changes

## Page 6

Enthalpy change, ΔH

 • ΔH means the change in enthalpy during a reaction.
 • ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants).
 • If products are lower in enthalpy than reactants, ΔH is negative.
 • If products are higher in enthalpy than reactants, ΔH is positive.
 • ΔH is commonly expressed in kJ mol⁻¹ when quoted per mole of reaction.

## Page 9

A12.3.2 Conservation of energy

 • Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it is transferred between the reacting
 system and the surroundings.
 • A chemical reaction may transfer energy to the surroundings or take in energy
 from the surroundings.
 • This direction of transfer distinguishes exothermic and endothermic changes.





A12.3 Enthalpy changes

## Page 10

Exothermic changes

 • An exothermic reaction transfers energy from the reacting system to the
 surroundings.
 • The products have lower enthalpy than the reactants.
 • ΔH is negative because H(products) − H(reactants) < 0.
 • The surroundings commonly become warmer.





A12.3 Enthalpy changes

## Page 11

Endothermic changes

 • An endothermic reaction takes in energy from the surroundings.
 • The products have higher enthalpy than the reactants.
 • ΔH is positive because H(products) − H(reactants) > 0.
 • The surroundings commonly become cooler.

## Page 16

Reading ΔH from a diagram

 • ΔH is the vertical difference between product and reactant enthalpy levels.
 • Use ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants).
 • Products lower than reactants → negative ΔH → exothermic.
 • Products higher than reactants → positive ΔH → endothermic.
 • The sign describes direction of enthalpy change, not whether activation energy
 is required.

## Page 17

Activation energy, Ea

 • Activation energy is the minimum energy required for a reaction to occur.
 • On an enthalpy diagram, Ea is represented by the energy difference from the
 reactant level to the top of the reaction pathway.
 • Both exothermic and endothermic reactions have an activation-energy barrier.

## Page 19

What a catalyst does and does
not do
 • A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation
 energy.
 • Lower Ea means a greater proportion of collisions can have sufficient energy
 to react.
 • A catalyst does not change the enthalpy of the reactants or products.
 • Therefore a catalyst does not change ΔH.

## Page 22

Error analysis: key corrections


    • Exothermic products must be below reactants and ΔH must be negative.
    • Endothermic products must be above reactants and ΔH must be positive.
    • Ea is measured from the reactant enthalpy level to the pathway peak.
    • A catalysed pathway has a lower peak but the same reactant and product levels,
   so ΔH is unchanged.

## Page 23

Common misconceptions

 • 'Exothermic means no activation energy.' Incorrect an exothermic reaction can
  still require Ea.
 • 'A catalyst makes ΔH smaller.' Incorrect it lowers Ea, not ΔH.
 • 'Endothermic means energy is destroyed.' Incorrect energy is transferred from
 the surroundings to the reacting system.
 • 'A negative ΔH means negative energy.' It means the products are lower in
 enthalpy than the reactants.
